Every product says it cares about your privacy. The sentence costs nothing to write. So instead of asking companies to promise harder, it's worth asking a different question: **what does the architecture allow?**

## Promise-based privacy vs. structural privacy

A promise-based product stores your data on its servers and pledges to behave. Structural privacy inverts that: the product is built so misbehaving isn't possible.

- If your data never reaches a server, it can't be sold, leaked from a breach, or subpoenaed from one.
- If cloud backup is encrypted on your device before upload, the host stores a blob it cannot read.
- If deletion is local, "delete my data" means the data is gone — not flagged for eventual removal in someone's pipeline.

That's what local-first means in practice. Not a marketing adjective — a set of things that are true because of where the bytes live.

## Where AI fits without breaking the model

Remem uses AI narrowly: drafting genuinely open-ended answers like cover letters. That requires a server, so the boundary is explicit — the context you choose to send is used for the draft and not persisted. Your memory itself never syncs to us. Deterministic fields never involve a model at all; your address is a fact, not a prediction.

## Verifiable beats trustworthy

The Remem extension is open source. That's not a growth tactic — it's the honest consequence of the argument above. If privacy claims are structural, the structure should be inspectable. You shouldn't have to trust our blog post; you can read the code that makes the blog post true.

Never repeat yourself. And never hand over the things you'd be repeating.
