Browser autofill was designed in an era of simple forms: a name, an email, a credit card. For those five fields, it's fine. Then you open a job application with forty questions spread across six steps, and it fills your email into the "referral source" box and calls it a day.

## Where autofill breaks

Autofill works by matching field names against a small, fixed vocabulary. That assumption fails constantly:

- **Nonstandard labels.** "Legal first name", "Given name", and "First name" are the same question — autofill treats them as strangers.
- **Multi-step forms.** ATS platforms split applications across pages. Autofill has no memory of the journey.
- **Structured history.** Employment entries, education records, references — autofill has nowhere to even store these.
- **Open-ended questions.** "Why do you want to work here?" No stored string will ever answer that.

## What a browser memory does instead

A browser memory starts from a different premise: instead of guessing what a field wants, it understands the form — then draws on a structured, local profile of you.

- Deterministic fields (name, address, dates, IDs) are matched with deterministic logic and filled **exactly** as you saved them. No model in the loop, no guessing.
- Open-ended questions get an AI-drafted answer from your real history — shown to you for review and rewriting before it touches the page.
- Nothing is ever submitted for you. You review, you click, you send.

## The part autofill got right

To be fair, built-in autofill got one thing right: your data stays in your browser. Any real alternative has to preserve that. Remem stores your memory encrypted, on your device, with export and delete always one action away. Cloud backup exists — optional and end-to-end encrypted — but the product never requires it.

The goal isn't to automate your browser. It's to stop the web from making you repeat yourself.
